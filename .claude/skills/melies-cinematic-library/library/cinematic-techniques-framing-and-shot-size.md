### Extreme Close-Up (ECU)

**Definition:** An extreme close-up, or extreme close up shot, fills the frame with one small feature: an eye, a mouth, a fingertip, a trigger. Surrounding space disappears; the crop is tighter than a whole face.

**Prompt:** Extreme close-up of [Subject], a single feature filling the frame, pores and texture, tighter than forehead-to-chin.

### Choker Shot (BCU)

**Definition:** A choker shot, also called a big close-up (BCU), frames the face from hairline to chin, often clipping the skull on every side. Shoulders are out; almost no background remains.

**Prompt:** Choker shot of [Subject] from hairline to chin, almost no background.

### Close-Up (CU)

**Definition:** A close-up, or close up shot, frames a face tightly enough to privilege detail over surroundings, usually head and upper shoulders. The room becomes secondary; the person still reads as a head, not a fragment.

**Prompt:** Tight close-up of [Subject], head and upper shoulders, 75mm, shallow depth of field.

### Medium Close-Up (MCU)

**Definition:** A medium close-up, or medium close up, usually frames a person from chest or shoulders upward. Dialogue distance remains: face, shoulders, and often a hand at chest height stay readable.

**Prompt:** Medium close-up of [Subject] from chest up, dialogue distance, shoulders and hands still readable, 65 to 75mm.

### Medium Shot (MS)

**Definition:** A medium shot frames a person from approximately the waist upward, so face and gesture share the rectangle. It is the workhorse size of coverage, sometimes called a waist shot.

**Prompt:** Medium shot of [Subject] from the waist up, conversational distance, 50mm, gesture and face in balance.

### Cowboy Shot

**Definition:** A cowboy shot frames a person from mid-thigh or the knees upward so the hands stay in at the holster line. It is also called an American shot or knee shot; it is not a waist shot and not a full figure.

**Prompt:** Cowboy shot of [Subject] framed mid-thigh up, hands visible at the holster line, American shot.

### Full Body (WS)

**Definition:** A full body shot holds a person head to toe, with the figure complete and a readable strip of environment around the silhouette. The person is still the subject; the world is a border, not the owner of the frame.

**Prompt:** Full-body shot of [Subject], head to toe, figure complete in frame, environment readable around the silhouette.

### Long Shot (WS)

**Definition:** A long shot in cinematography shows the full subject plus a substantial portion of the environment, so a person sits small in geography. The figure is still locatable as a human; the world is large enough to ignore them.

**Prompt:** Long shot of [Subject] small in a large environment, full figure plus geography.

### Extreme Long Shot (ELS)

**Definition:** An extreme long shot makes environment, scale, geography, or isolation dominant over individual detail. A person, if present, is a speck: a scale mark, not a portrait.

**Prompt:** Extreme long shot of [Subject] as a speck against vast terrain, infinite depth of field, landscape as the true subject.

### Establishing Shot

**Definition:** An establishing shot introduces or re-establishes location, geography, time of day, or social context. It is a job in the sequence, not a synonym for wide or extreme long shot.

**Prompt:** Wide establishing shot of [Subject] and the location at once, time of day and spatial relations declared before coverage begins.

### Master Shot

**Definition:** A master shot covers the complete dramatic action and the spatial geography of a scene in one setup. It is the skeleton you can play in full, then cut into.

**Prompt:** Wide master of [Subject] with all blocking visible, the skeleton of the scene in one continuous frame.

### Two-Shot

**Definition:** A two-shot, or two shot, frames two principal subjects together so their distance and posture can be compared in one composition. The space between them is part of the sentence.

**Prompt:** Two-shot of [Subject] with a second figure, the space between them doing narrative work, 50mm.

### Three-Shot

**Definition:** A three-shot, or three shot, frames three principal subjects together, usually so alliances and the odd one out are readable in the blocking. It is a group sentence of three, not three singles cut together.

**Prompt:** Three-shot of [Subject] in a triangular group, alliances and the odd one out readable in blocking.

### Group Shot

**Definition:** A group shot frames a small ensemble as a relational unit, such as a family, a crew, or a jury. Position, height, and light say who belongs and who dominates.

**Prompt:** Group shot containing [Subject] among several figures, ensemble blocking, who owns the frame by position and light.

### Insert Shot

**Definition:** An insert shot is a close view of a story-relevant detail: an object, a hand action, a screen, or text. It points at the thing that will matter, at real scale.

**Prompt:** Tight close-up of an object that carries the plot near [Subject]: a glass, a letter, a latch.

### Cutaway

**Definition:** A cutaway shot leaves the primary action for a related detail, observer, or part of the same world. It is used for context, reaction, compression, or continuity repair.

**Prompt:** Cutaway from [Subject] to a related detail in the same world (a clock, a kettle, a street, a listener), not an insert on the actor.

### Reaction Shot

**Definition:** A reaction shot shows a character's response to an event, a line, or an unseen stimulus. The important action is watching; the face (or body) is the cut.

**Prompt:** Reaction close-up of [Subject] receiving news they cannot speak yet, the face as the cut.

### Over-the-Shoulder Coverage (OTS)

**Definition:** Over-the-shoulder coverage, or an over the shoulder shot, puts a near shoulder or head in the foreground while the far face stays sharp. Paired reverses preserve relational space.

**Prompt:** Over-the-shoulder framing of [Subject], near shoulder soft in foreground, the far face sharp, looking toward the near shoulder, 180-line held.

### Single

**Definition:** A single, in single shot cinematography, is a coverage shot of one principal character, not a one-take scene. A clean single keeps other bodies out; a dirty single lets a shoulder or blur from the partner chew the edge without becoming a true over-the-shoulder.

**Prompt:** Clean single of [Subject], one person, no foreground shoulder, the frame belonging entirely to them.

### Cut-ins

**Definition:** A cut-in, or cut in shot, moves from a wider view to a closer detail of something already present in that space, usually along the same axis. It proves a fact the wide cannot prove: the hands, the phone, the wound.

**Prompt:** Cut-in on a detail of [Subject] already in the master, tighter, the same axis, a fact the wide cannot prove.

### Gesture

**Definition:** Gesture cinematography sizes and times a shot around a discrete expressive body movement: a hand, a glance, a weight shift. The plot is in the motion, not in an object sitting still.

**Prompt:** Gesture close on [Subject], the meaning in a hand, a glance, a weight shift, coverage of the small action.

### Interview

**Definition:** Interview cinematography is a talking-head setup: stable size, eyeline just off the lens, a listener implied. Someone is answering a question we often do not hear.

**Prompt:** Interview frame of [Subject], talking-head distance, eyeline just off lens, a listener implied.

### Product

**Definition:** Product cinematography lights and frames an object the way a portrait treats a face: hero angle, catalog clarity, controlled reflections. The thing is the subject, not a plot insert in a messy room.

**Prompt:** Product hero of [Subject], catalog clarity, controlled reflections, the object as the only subject.

### Video Portraits

**Definition:** A video portrait is a sustained, simply covered study of one person: stable size, patient time, the face as the whole event. Duration is the intimacy; a person is enough.

**Prompt:** Video portrait of [Subject], a held face, duration as intimacy.

### Wide Shot (WS)

**Definition:** A wide shot shows the subject in full environment, with bodies small enough that place still speaks. It is environment-forward coverage: do not isolate yet.

**Prompt:** Wide shot of [Subject] in full environment, bodies small enough that place still speaks.
