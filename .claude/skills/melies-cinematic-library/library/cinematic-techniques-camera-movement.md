### Slow Zoom In

**Definition:** A slow zoom in is slow zoom in cinematography that tightens the frame by increasing focal length while the camera body stays locked. Perspective relations stay fixed: the world does not slide, it only enlarges as the lens compresses space.

**Prompt:** Slow optical zoom in on [Subject], camera body locked on a heavy tripod, lens gradually tightening over several seconds, background compression increasing, no physical dolly, prestige-drama patience. For motion: the lens slowly zooms in for 6 seconds while the camera body stays locked and the subject stays centered.

### Slow Zoom Out

**Definition:** A slow zoom out widens the frame by decreasing focal length while the support stays put. More architecture and negative space enter the picture, yet the camera never travels, so true parallax does not appear.

**Prompt:** Slow optical zoom out from [Subject], tripod locked, the frame breathing open to reveal surrounding architecture and negative space, no camera body travel. For motion: the lens slowly zooms out for 6 seconds from a tight frame on [Subject] into a wider environment.

### Crash Zoom In

**Definition:** A crash zoom is a rapid focal-length snap from a wider frame into a detail, usually without moving the camera. The punch is optical and conspicuous: size changes faster than a body can walk.

**Prompt:** Aggressive crash zoom into [Subject], snap optical punch-in under one second, radial motion blur, Shaw Brothers energy, then a hard hold on the tight frame. For motion: sudden crash zoom into [Subject] in under a second, then hold.

### Crash Zoom Out

**Definition:** A crash zoom out is a sudden, fast widening of the lens from a tight frame into a much wider one, usually without moving the camera. It dumps the subject back into the environment in a fraction of a second.

**Prompt:** Sudden crash zoom out from [Subject], the environment exploding into view in a fraction of a second, then a locked wide hold. For motion: snap zoom out from [Subject] in under a second, revealing the wider scene, then hold.

### Dolly In

**Definition:** A dolly in shot moves the entire camera through space on a wheeled or tracked support, advancing toward the subject at a measured pace. The nodal point walks, so foreground and background shift in parallax as the surroundings are progressively excluded.

**Prompt:** Physical dolly in toward [Subject] on track, camera body gliding forward, parallax shifting foreground against background, 40mm spherical, buttery traction. For motion: the camera physically dollies forward toward [Subject] for 6 seconds.

### Dolly Out

**Definition:** A dolly out shot withdraws the entire camera through space on a wheeled or tracked support. The world widens with true parallax, and the subject shrinks inside architecture because the station point has actually retreated.

**Prompt:** Physical dolly out from [Subject], camera retreating on track, the world widening with true parallax, subject shrinking inside architecture. For motion: the camera physically dollies backward away from [Subject], revealing more environment with parallax.

### Dolly Left

**Definition:** A dolly left, also called a truck left, slides the whole camera sideways to the left on wheels or track. The lens stays aimed with the subject while background planes peel past in parallax.

**Prompt:** Lateral dolly left across [Subject], camera sliding sideways on track, background planes peeling in parallax, subject tracked in frame. For motion: the camera slides left on a dolly, keeping [Subject] in frame, background parallax visible.

### Dolly Right

**Definition:** A dolly right, or truck right, moves the camera body sideways to the right without panning. New space enters from one side of the frame while the other side exits, and layered parallax proves the translation.

**Prompt:** Lateral dolly right across [Subject], smooth sideways travel, layered parallax, locked horizon. For motion: the camera slides right on a dolly, keeping [Subject] framed, smooth lateral parallax.

### Pan Left

**Definition:** A pan left is pan shot cinematography that rotates the camera on a fixed tripod or head around its vertical axis, sweeping the view to the left. The support does not travel; only the direction of the lens changes.

**Prompt:** Slow pan left from a locked tripod across [Subject], camera rotating on its vertical axis, horizon level, revealing new architecture to the left. For motion: the camera pans left from a fixed tripod, sweeping across [Subject] and the environment.

### Pan Right

**Definition:** A pan right shot is a horizontal rotation of a locked-off camera toward the right. It connects two parts of the same geography in one continuous turn from a single station point.

**Prompt:** Slow pan right from a locked tripod across [Subject], fluid head rotation, level horizon, a continuous horizontal reveal. For motion: the camera pans right from a fixed tripod across [Subject].

### Tilt Up

**Definition:** A tilt up shot rotates the camera vertically around a fixed position, climbing from a lower composition to a higher one. The tripod does not rise; only pitch changes, so the viewpoint height stays the same.

**Prompt:** Slow tilt up from [Subject], camera rotating on its horizontal axis, climbing from ground detail to face or from face to towering architecture. For motion: the camera tilts up from a fixed position, starting low on [Subject] and climbing vertically.

### Tilt Down

**Definition:** A tilt-down rotates the camera vertically from a fixed position, starting higher and ending lower. It can drop from sky or architecture onto a person or object without moving the tripod.

**Prompt:** Slow tilt down onto [Subject], starting in sky or ceiling and descending to the figure, a measured vertical scan. For motion: the camera tilts down from above onto [Subject], revealing the figure from head to environment.

### Whip Pan

**Definition:** A whip pan pans so quickly that the image blurs into horizontal streaks, then snaps to a new composition. Two shots can be joined inside compatible directional motion blur, creating the impression of one fast reorientation.

**Prompt:** Violent whip pan off [Subject], horizontal motion blur streaking the world into ribbons, then a snap resolve on the next beat. For motion: extremely fast whip pan away from [Subject], heavy horizontal motion blur, then a sharp stop.

### Whip Tilt

**Definition:** A whip tilt is a violent, motion-blurred vertical rotation of the camera, then a hard stop. It is the up-or-down cousin of a whip pan: shock through speed, not through traveling the support.

**Prompt:** Violent vertical whip tilt across [Subject], the frame ripping up or down with motion blur, then a hard stop. For motion: extremely fast vertical whip tilt across [Subject] with motion blur, then a hard stop.

### Pedestal Up

**Definition:** A pedestal shot moves the entire camera vertically without changing its tilt. The lens rises on a column, eyeline climbs, and architecture slides down the frame while pitch stays constant.

**Definition:** A pedestal down lowers the entire camera vertically without changing its tilt. The descent stays on one axis: a calm drop through the same look, not a nod and not a crane swoop.

### Crane Up

**Definition:** A crane shot lifts the camera on a jib or Technocrane, often easing backward at the same time, so the frame climbs from a human scale into a wider view of the location. Height change is the point, and a little boom-out keeps the subject from sliding off the bottom of the frame.

**Prompt:** Majestic crane up from [Subject], camera rising and easing backward on a Technocrane, the individual becoming one figure inside a vast environment, golden or blue-hour air, 35mm Signature Prime. For motion: the camera cranes smoothly upward and slightly backward from shoulder height, rising above [Subject] to reveal the true surrounding scale.

### Crane Down

**Definition:** A crane down shot lowers the camera on an arm from a high wide view into a human-scale frame. The world collapses from map to face because the lens occupies new heights, not because it merely nods.

**Prompt:** Crane down onto [Subject], starting high and descending into a human-scale frame, the world collapsing from map to face. For motion: the camera cranes down from a high wide view into a closer frame on [Subject].

### Crane Over

**Definition:** A crane-over lifts and arcs the camera on a jib or crane until it looks down on the scene from above. Height and angle change together in one move, turning the floor into a map.

**Prompt:** Crane over [Subject], the arm rising and arcing forward until the camera looks nearly straight down, a continuous climb into overhead. For motion: the camera cranes up and over [Subject], finishing in a high downward angle.

### Jib Shot

**Prompt:** Short jib move on [Subject], a compact crane arm lifting or sweeping a few feet, more intimate than a full Technocrane, elegant vertical accent. For motion: a compact jib arm lifts a few feet above [Subject] with a gentle sweep.

### Tracking Shot

### Follow Shot

**Definition:** A follow shot trails a moving subject at a fixed offset, usually from behind. The back of the head becomes foreground and the world opens ahead, so pursuit, haunting, or arrival is felt as walking into space.

**Prompt:** Follow shot behind [Subject], camera trailing at a fixed offset, the back of the head as foreground, the world opening ahead. For motion: the camera follows behind [Subject], matching their movement through the space.

### Steadicam

### Handheld

### Dutch Roll
