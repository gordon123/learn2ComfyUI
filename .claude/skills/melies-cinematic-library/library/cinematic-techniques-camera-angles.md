# Cinematic Techniques — Camera Angles (19 เทคนิก)

### Eye Level

**Definition:** An eye-level shot places the camera at the subject's eye height with a horizontal sightline and a level horizon. It is the default equal relationship: height and tilt assign no rank until you leave this setup.

**Prompt:** Eye-level shot of [Subject], camera at their eye height, horizontal sightline, equal relationship, no power tilt.

### High Angle

**Definition:** A high-angle shot looks downward at the subject from above eye level, an oblique pitch rather than a straight drop. More floor enters the frame, the figure occupies less of the rectangle, and the viewpoint reads as observation from above.

**Prompt:** High-angle shot looking down on [Subject], the figure diminished, architecture or sky weighing on them.

### Low Angle

**Definition:** A low-angle shot looks upward at the subject from below eye level, so verticals converge and ceilings or sky take weight. The camera is not merely low; it is pitched up, and that pitch is what assigns monument or intimidation.

**Prompt:** Low-angle shot looking up at [Subject], converging verticals, heroic or oppressive height, Citizen Kane floor.

### Bird's-Eye View

**Definition:** A bird's-eye view shot looks steeply or directly down from a great height so people and furniture become a map. Height, not just distance, is the point of view: life reads as a board game or a ritual seen from above.

**Prompt:** Bird's-eye view straight down on [Subject], camera over one seated person and a table, floor as a map, no extra limbs.

### Worm's-Eye View

**Definition:** A worm's-eye view looks sharply upward from ground level or below the subject, an extreme low pitch that collapses verticals inward. The subject is a tower and the camera is dust.

**Prompt:** Worm's-eye view from ground level looking straight up at [Subject], extreme low, verticals collapsing inward, awe or terror.

### Overhead Top-Down

**Definition:** An overhead top-down shot looks nearly straight down at 90 degrees, flattening bodies and spaces into a graphic arrangement. Faces are scarce; silhouette, props, and the plan of the table or room do the talking.

**Prompt:** Overhead top-down shot of [Subject] at 90 degrees, flat-lay or room plan, shadows as drawing.

### Ground Level

**Definition:** A ground-level shot puts the lens almost on the floor, so boots, hems, wheels, and the dirt become actors. Height is the fact: the camera starts from the ground plane, whether it looks along the street or steeply up.

**Prompt:** Ground-level camera looking steeply up at [Subject], lens nearly on the floor, boots and trailing coat monumental.

### Dutch Angle

**Definition:** A Dutch angle rolls the camera so verticals and the horizon appear tilted. The cant is usually static: gravity's down is no longer the bottom of the frame, which is why the world reads as off its axis.

**Prompt:** Dutch angle of [Subject], camera rolled 20 to 30 degrees, diagonal horizon, moral or mental tilt.

### Point of View

**Definition:** A POV shot makes the camera function as a character's optical viewpoint: what they see at their eye height. The looker's head and shoulder stay out of the frame; we inhabit the look, not a seat behind it.

**Prompt:** What [Subject] sees: the world at their eye height, no back of a head, no shoulder of the looker in frame.

### Profile

**Definition:** A profile shot places the camera at 90 degrees to the head so one ear, the jaw, and the nose read as architecture, with the look offscreen. It is a true side view, not a three-quarter face and not a stamped coin if the living head still breathes.

**Prompt:** True profile of [Subject], camera at 90 degrees to the head, one ear, the nose as architecture, looking offscreen.

### Three-Quarter Angle

**Definition:** A three-quarter angle turns the face about 45 degrees to the camera so both eyes remain visible and the far cheek recedes. It is the standard narrative portrait angle: dimensionality without a full front or a true side.

**Prompt:** Three-quarter angle on [Subject], face turned about 45 degrees, classic portrait dimensionality.

### Reverse Angle

**Definition:** A reverse-angle shot views the same spatial relationship from the opposing side, completing the axis of a look, a dialogue, or a standoff. Geography flips; the 180-degree line is respected so screen direction still matches.

**Prompt:** Reverse angle on [Subject], the opposing view of a dialogue or standoff, 180-line respected, geography flipping.

### Trunk Shot

**Definition:** A trunk shot places the camera inside a car trunk or similar confined pit, looking up at the people who open it, with the lid and rim framing their downward looks. It is a located low viewpoint, not a generic look-up in a parking lot.

**Prompt:** Trunk shot looking up at [Subject] from inside a car trunk or similar confined pit, playful low-angle signature.

### Hip Level

**Definition:** A hip-level shot sits the camera around belt or holster height so hands, gait, and weapons share the plane of focus. It is a height choice, usually with a level horizon: the hands will decide.

**Prompt:** Hip-level camera on [Subject], gunbelt height, Western stance, hands and holster in the plane of focus.

### Shoulder Level

**Definition:** A shoulder-level camera sits slightly below the eyes, at the height of a walking companion. It is less formal than true Eye Level and less monumental than a Low Angle; the face is still the target, seen from the deltoid rather than the pupil.

**Prompt:** Shoulder-level camera on [Subject], slightly below the eyes, a walking companion's view, less formal than true eye-level.

### First-Person

**Definition:** A first-person camera makes the lens function as a character's bodily viewpoint, with a body that owns it: hands entering the frame, breath, tools, locomotion. It is subjective seeing as continuous embodiment, not a single optical insert.

**Prompt:** First-person camera as [Subject] would see, hands entering frame, breath, a body that owns the lens.

### Fourth Wall

**Definition:** A fourth-wall look aims the subject's eyes into the taking lens, so the scene admits an audience. The camera is no longer an invisible observer; it is a person in the room, or the spectator the character has decided to include.

**Prompt:** Fourth-wall look from [Subject], eyes into the lens, the scene admitting an audience.

### Object POV

**Definition:** An object-POV shot is the viewpoint of a tool, a ball, a bullet, a phone, or a vehicle: the prop has eyes. The camera is parented to that object, so the object stays stable in the frame while the world moves relative to it.

**Prompt:** Object POV as if [Subject] were seen from a tool, a ball, a bullet, a phone: the thing looking.

### Incline

**Definition:** An incline is a slight camera roll, a gentle cant of the floor rather than a full Dutch angle. The horizon is a few degrees off true, enough to suggest slope or unease without declaring that the world has fallen over.

**Prompt:** Inclined camera on [Subject], a gentle cant, not a full Dutch, the floor suggesting slope or unease.
