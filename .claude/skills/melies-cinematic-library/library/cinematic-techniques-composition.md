### Rule of Thirds

**Definition:** The rule of thirds in rule of thirds cinematography divides the frame into nine equal cells and parks important masses on those lines or at their four intersections. The horizon usually sits on an upper or lower third so land and sky are not equal partners, and the subject usually sits off-center with look-space in the larger remaining field.

**Prompt:** Rule-of-thirds placement of [Subject] on a power intersection, horizon on a third, breathing room in the look direction.

### Golden Ratio

**Definition:** Golden ratio composition organizes the frame with proportional relationships near phi, about eight to five, as a phi grid or a logarithmic spiral rather than a 3x3. The main mass sits closer to center than a third, and the eye is asked to travel a curve through secondary masses instead of jumping cell to cell.

**Prompt:** Golden-ratio spiral composition around [Subject], the eye traveling a harmonic curve rather than a grid.

### Central Framing

**Definition:** Central framing, also called dead center or center punch, places the figure on the optical axis so the body sits on the crosshairs of the frame. Bilateral weight may gather around that body, but the first fact is placement: the person is the altar, not a guest in a third.

**Prompt:** Central framing of [Subject], the figure on the optical axis, bilateral weight, a formal altar in the frame.

### Centered Composition

**Definition:** Centered composition places the dominant subject on or near the central vertical axis and organizes the rest of the picture around that axis. The frame reads as a frontal tableau: walls, furniture, and attendants arranged so the subject is an altar, not an accident of blocking.

**Prompt:** Dead-center composition of [Subject], bilateral symmetry or frontal tableau, Wes Anderson formality, the subject as altar.

### Symmetry

**Definition:** Symmetry in a symmetrical composition balances visual masses around an axis, usually the vertical centerline, so left answers right. The world looks designed, or it is pretending to be, whether or not a person occupies the middle.

**Prompt:** Perfect symmetry around [Subject], mirrored architecture, a central axis, obsessive order.

### Asymmetry

**Definition:** Asymmetry, or asymmetrical composition, offsets the dominant mass and answers it with a different kind of weight: color, light, a small face, a dark void, or a distant doorway. The frame does not mirror, so leftover space is a counterweight rather than a matching wing.

**Prompt:** Asymmetrical balance with [Subject] offset, counterweights of color or mass, tension instead of mirror.

### Leading Lines

**Definition:** Leading lines in leading lines cinematography are edges, rails, roads, corridors, shadows, or gestures that steer the eye toward or through the image. The lines are vectors in the diagram, not decoration around a centered face.

**Prompt:** Leading lines driving the eye to [Subject], rails, corridors, roads, or shadows converging on the figure.

### Frame within Frame

**Definition:** A frame within a frame (doorway framing, surcadrage) puts a second rectangle inside the screen, a door, window, arch, mirror, or gap that encloses the subject. The inner aperture is a second boundary, and crossing it is a beat, not a decoration.

**Prompt:** Frame-within-frame around [Subject], doorway, window, mirror, or arch as a second rectangle, nested attention.

### Negative Space

**Definition:** Negative space in negative space cinematography leaves a large, relatively empty area around or beside the subject so absence becomes a mass. The unoccupied field is still a world (sky, wall, snow, sea), and the subject occupies a small or unusual portion of the diagram.

**Prompt:** Vast negative space around [Subject], the figure small, absence as weight, Antonioni emptiness.

### Foreground Interest

**Definition:** Foreground interest (a foreground anchor or occluded observation) puts a salient near object between the lens and the subject: flowers, a fence, glass, a table, or a body. The near layer creates depth, scale, a visual entry, or partial concealment, so air and obstacle sit between us and them.

**Prompt:** Strong foreground object between lens and [Subject], dirty aperture of flowers, fence, or glass, layered depth.

### Layered Depth

**Definition:** Layered depth, or layered composition, stages important action on at least two distances, typically foreground, midground, and background in one picture. Depth staging is that arrangement, while deep focus is a separate optical choice about how much of it is sharp.

**Prompt:** Three-plane composition around [Subject], foreground, mid, and deep background all carrying information, deep staging.

### Diagonal Composition

**Definition:** Diagonal composition uses diagonal lines and masses (stairs, rifles, rain, a body on the bias, a street receding on a corner) to raise energy, instability, or depth. The camera can stay level, because the set supplies the tilt of the diagram.

**Prompt:** Diagonal composition through [Subject], energy on the bias, unstable, rain or stairs as the line.

### Triangular Composition

**Definition:** Triangular composition arranges subjects or visual masses into a triangle so three points lock the frame. A group gets a hierarchy: the apex is usually power, witness, or the object of desire, and the base is support, threat, or the audience inside the picture.

**Prompt:** Triangular arrangement with [Subject] at an apex, three masses locking the frame, Renaissance blocking.

### Headroom

**Definition:** Headroom in headroom cinematography is the space between the crown of the head and the top frame edge. Tight headroom applies pressure, while generous headroom hands the upper field to air, architecture, or sky.

**Prompt:** Deliberate headroom on [Subject], either tight to the hair (pressure) or generously open (air, God, sky).

### Lead Room

**Definition:** Lead room in lead room cinematography (nose room, looking room) is space in the direction a subject looks or moves. More frame sits ahead of the walk or the gaze than behind the head, so the surplus is the path rather than leftover wallpaper.

**Prompt:** Lead room in front of [Subject]'s look or walk, more space in the direction of attention than behind, forward narrative.

### Short Siding

**Definition:** Short siding places the face near the frame edge the character looks toward, crushing look-space and leaving surplus air behind the head. The edge they face becomes a wall, so the diagram denies them a future in the picture.

**Prompt:** Short-sided framing of [Subject], look space crushed, the face near the frame edge they look toward, trapped or urgent.

### Visual Weight

**Definition:** Visual weight in visual weight composition is the attention an element attracts through size, contrast, color, position, focus, faces, or motion. A small bright thing can equal a large dark mass, because scale of attention is not scale of object.

**Prompt:** Composition balancing visual weight around [Subject], a bright small thing equal to a large dark mass, scale of attention.

### Repetition and Pattern

**Definition:** Repetition and pattern composition repeats shapes, colors, lines, positions, or actions so the eye reads a system of columns, windows, soldiers, or hotel doors. A break in that pattern is often the story, because the grid had already set the expectation.

**Prompt:** Repeating pattern around [Subject], columns, windows, soldiers, a break in the pattern as the story.

### Figure-Ground

**Definition:** Figure-ground composition separates the subject from the field so the person or object reads in under a second. Contrast of tone, color, or focus does the split, and if figure and ground share the same value the diagram fails even if the acting is good.

**Prompt:** Clear figure-ground separation of [Subject] from background, contrast of tone, color, or focus so the person reads instantly.

### Tonal Contrast

**Definition:** Tonal contrast, the contrast composition of lightest against darkest mass, organizes the frame as a fight of value with the subject as one of those poles. Graphic punch replaces mid-grey mush, so the diagram reads as a poster of value rather than a gentle range of detail.

**Prompt:** High tonal contrast composition with [Subject] as the lightest or darkest mass, graphic punch, no mid-gray mush.

### One-Point Perspective

**Definition:** One-point perspective in one point perspective cinematography squares the camera to a plane so receding parallels meet at a single vanishing point, often on the central axis. The result is tunnel depth or ceremonial order: a corridor, a nave, a hotel hall aimed at infinity or at a person.

**Prompt:** One-point perspective corridor toward [Subject] or through them, vanishing point on axis, Kubrick infinity.

### Vanishing Point

**Definition:** A vanishing point in vanishing point composition is the apparent meeting place of parallel lines in perspective, a destination for rails, a road, or a colonnade. The person often stands as a gate in front of that meeting, or occupies it as the target of the architecture.

**Prompt:** Deep vanishing point beyond [Subject], rails or road sucking the eye to infinity, the person as a gate.

### Dirty Frame

**Definition:** A dirty frame in dirty frame cinematography lets out-of-focus shoulders, glass, foliage, or crowd chew the edges of the picture so the aperture is occupied. The camera sits inside a populated space, not on a seamless studio axis.

**Prompt:** Dirty frame around [Subject], out-of-focus shoulders, glass, foliage, or crowd chewing the edges, voyeur layers.

### Clean Frame

**Definition:** A clean frame in clean frame cinematography keeps the edges empty of accidental objects so isolation arrives by tidiness, not by blur. The diagram has no landlord in the corners, which is studio-poster clarity even on a real street.

**Prompt:** Clean frame of [Subject], edges empty, no accidental objects, studio-poster clarity, isolation by tidiness.

### Look Space

**Definition:** Look space framing is the field in front of a gaze, the air a look owns, often because the important object is off-screen. In cutting, that look may be joined to what is seen (raccord regard, the look-object relationship).

**Prompt:** Generous look space for [Subject], the off-screen gaze owning a field of air, tension in what we cannot see.

### Architexture

**Definition:** Architexture, a nickname for architectural cinematography that treats the set as subject, fills the frame with facades, grids, and materials until a person is a detail of the building. Texture, module, and mass own space, and the body is an ornament or a scale figure.

**Prompt:** Architecture as texture around [Subject], facades, grids, and materials filling the frame until the person is a detail of the building.

### Reflections

**Definition:** Reflections in reflection cinematography put observer and observed in one plane by using glass, water, chrome, or a mirror. Mirror dialogue and one-way-glass staging let two spaces occupy a single composition, because optics, not a filter, do the doubling.

**Prompt:** Reflections of [Subject] in glass, water, or chrome, observer and observed in one plane.

### Screen in Screen

**Definition:** Screen in screen (desktop film, interface as the camera) nests a monitor, phone, television, or cinema inside the cinema so someone is watching a picture of someone. The inner image has its own crop, delay, and artifacts, and nested looking is the diagram.

**Prompt:** Screen-in-screen of [Subject], a monitor, phone, or cinema inside the cinema, nested looking.

### Tableau

**Definition:** A tableau (tableau vivant, frontal ensemble, or frieze) is a staged composition whose meaning develops inside a relatively fixed frame. Arranged bodies and objects form a living picture, so duration replaces coverage while the camera stays locked or nearly locked.

**Prompt:** Tableau of [Subject], arranged bodies, a living painting, duration instead of coverage.

### Void

**Definition:** A void in void cinematography erases the set: darkness, white, or a blank field eating the world until a figure stands against nothing. Negative space still belongs to a place, while a void deletes place, leaving almost no landlord but the body or the darkness that might eat it.

**Prompt:** Void around [Subject], darkness or blank field eating the world, a figure against nothing.

### Voyeur

**Definition:** Voyeur cinematography places the camera in concealment so the audience is trespassing: a view through foliage, glass, a grate, or a distant window, often with dirty edges. The diagram is a peephole, not coverage, and the owner of that hide does not want us there.

**Prompt:** Voyeur frame of [Subject], watching from concealment, dirty edges, a view we should not have.

### Windows

**Definition:** Windows cinematography treats panes as the true subject: stacked apertures, lives in rectangles, a building of frames. The diagram is a grid of possible pictures, not one doorway around one face.

**Prompt:** Windows as the true subject around [Subject], stacked apertures, lives in rectangles, a building of frames.
